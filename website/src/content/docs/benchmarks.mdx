These numbers measure the pool alone: a job is an `int` and the workers do nothing with it, so what's left is
the cost of queueing a job and handing it to a worker. Your jobs will dominate a real run; the point is that
the pool adds as little as possible.

`Delegate` alone runs into a queue with room for every job. A job through workers runs through a queue of 1024:
when the workers fall behind, `Delegate` waits, so the time per job is the pace of the whole pool. With 16
callers at once, the time is wall time per call across all of them, so it measures throughput.

Both versions ran on the same harness, with the same jobs.

v2 built a slice of `reflect.Value`s for every job, boxing each argument, and called the worker with
`reflect.Value.Call`. v3 sends the job on a typed channel and calls the worker's func directly.

With jobs that do nothing, one caller can't keep 16 workers busy: they wait and wake for every job, which
costs more than the job itself. With real work, the workers stay busy and take jobs straight from the queue.

<Aside title="Where the time goes">
`Delegate` checks the context, takes a read lock that orders it against `Stop`, and sends on a channel.
A worker checks whether it was removed, which takes no lock, and receives from the channel; only when the
queue is empty does it wait on both the queue and its quit channel. Neither allocates. Adding a worker
allocates the worker, its quit channel, its goroutine's closure and the `remove` func it returns.
</Aside>

## Run them yourself

From a clone of the repository:

```sh
go test -run '^$' -bench . -benchmem
```

To compare two versions, run each a few times and compare with [benchstat](https://pkg.go.dev/golang.org/x/perf/cmd/benchstat):

```sh
go test -run '^$' -bench . -count 10 > new.txt
benchstat old.txt new.txt
```

The allocation counts are also enforced by tests: `TestAllocations` fails if a call allocates more than it should.
